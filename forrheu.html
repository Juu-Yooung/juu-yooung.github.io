MOTIVATION & BACKGROUND

What happens
between appointments?

For me, this project began with my mother’s experience of rheumatoid arthritis.

After patients are diagnosed, regular clinical visits become part of their routine. Yet short consultations can make it difficult to describe the changes they experience in their painful finger joints between appointments.

I wanted to explore how a person could keep a more consistent record at home, and bring that history into the next conversation with their doctor.

How might we make regular self-measurement useful, understandable, and easy to make part of daily life?

The gap between clinical visits

Changes in symptoms can be hard to reconstruct during a short appointment.

The burden of measurement

A home device needs to respect painful hands and keep the user’s actions simple.

A record people can use

The sensing result needs a clear place in an ongoing record and a meaningful conversation.

Our four-person team brought mechanical engineering and industrial design into the same design process.

Our ambition was to connect technical feasibility with a product people could understand and use. How should a hand be positioned? How should contact be maintained? What should the screen explain, and how should a measurement become useful information?

We treated the sensing mechanism, physical form, interaction, and app as interdependent design decisions. User research shaped the engineering questions; prototypes and experiments, in turn, shaped the design.

BIOIMPEDANCE MEASUREMENT

From electrode contact
to a repeatable signal.

Bioimpedance measures how tissue opposes an alternating current. Fluid changes associated with inflammation motivated its use for joint monitoring.

ACQUISITIONTissue impedance

ACQUISITION & CONVERGENCE

Measuring a stable value.

A MAX30001G evaluation kit acquired bioimpedance at 8 μA and 128 kHz. MATLAB processing identified a stable window after the initial transient.

8 μADrive current128 kHzFrequency

Contact pressure is part of the sensing design.

Five pressures were compared in one healthy participant. The prototype selected 45.7 kPa, balancing convergence variation, time, and usability; broader clinical validation remains necessary.

PROTOTYPING & EVALUATION

User-centered
development.

The design evolved through conversations, physical mockups, functional prototypes, and expert feedback. Each stage connected a human need to a concrete design question.

Develop the mechanisms

Early prototypes connected the controller, relays, air pump, and hand-shaped cushion to explore a workable measurement sequence.

View the electronics, pneumatics, and electrode fixtures

Refine the form and interaction

Successive mockups helped us explore hand positioning, access, enclosure geometry, and the relationship between the user and the measurement hardware.

Integrate the final prototype

The final prototype brings the hand-insertion units, display, sensing electronics, and pneumatic mechanisms into one device.

Evaluate the whole experience

Feedback covered the physical interaction, screen guidance, and the usefulness of the information.

The name brings “For,” “Rheumatoid,” and “U” together. The identity expresses the same intention as the device: care that fits into the user’s daily life.
